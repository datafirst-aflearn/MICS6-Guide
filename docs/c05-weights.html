5.1 Which MICS weight should I use?

MICS provides different weights for different questionnaire populations.

File/population Standard weight
Household hhweight
Women wmweight
Men, where applicable mnweight
Children under five chweight
Selected children aged 5–17 fsweight

The MICS6 Manual for Statistical Data Analysis explains that questionnaire-specific weights are attached to the corresponding datasets.

5.1.1 For foundational-learning analysis, normally use fsweight

The FLS assessment is administered within the Questionnaire for Children Age 5–17, for which one eligible child is randomly selected per household.

That extra within-household selection step matters.

A child in a household containing several eligible 5–17-year-olds has a lower probability of being selected for fs than an otherwise comparable child who is the only eligible child in their household.

fsweight accounts for the selected-child sampling process.

Outcome: foundational numeracy
Population: children aged 7–14 in fs
Weight: fsweight

and:

Outcome: reading accuracy
Population: children who reached the reading passage
Base analysis file: fs
Weight: fsweight

The fact that the outcome is defined only for a subgroup does not mean a new questionnaire weight is required.

5.1.2 The weight follows the analysis population, not the source of each variable

Suppose you start with fs and merge household wealth from hh:

fs:
child learning outcome
child age
fsweight

        +

hh:
household wealth

        ↓

one row per selected child

The unit of analysis is still the selected child. Use fsweight, not hhweight.

Adding a parent’s education from hl or another linked file does not turn the analysis into a household-member analysis.

Ask “what does one row represent?”

That question is usually more useful than asking which file a covariate originally came from.

5.1.3 What is fshweight?

MICS6 fs files also commonly contain fshweight, labelled the Children 5–17 household sample weight.

This is distinct from fsweight.

For ordinary child-level analyses of the randomly selected 5–17-year-old — including FLS outcomes — use fsweight.

fsweight incorporates the selection of one child from the eligible 5–17-year-olds in the household, whereas the household-level weight does not represent each selected child with that within-household selection adjustment.

5.1.4 Do I need to divide the weight by 1,000 or another constant?

Not for standard estimates of means, proportions or regression coefficients.

Multiplying every weight in one survey by the same positive constant does not change a weighted mean or proportion. The MICS weights can therefore normally be supplied to survey software as released.

Do not rescale weights simply because another survey programme uses a particular scaling convention.

5.1.5 A note on population totals

MICS analysis weights are commonly normalised rather than expansion weights to a known national population total.

That means they are appropriate for estimating proportions, means and relationships, but the sum of weights should not automatically be interpreted as the number of people in the national population.

If the research question requires population counts or totals, consult the country sampling documentation and determine whether an additional population-calibration step is required.

5.1.6 Weighting does not replace the survey design

This is not enough:

weighted.mean(outcome, fsweight)

and this is not enough for inference:

mean outcome [pw=fsweight]

These can produce a weighted point estimate, but they do not fully specify the clustered and stratified MICS design.

For design-correct standard errors, set up the complete survey design as shown in Setting up one MICS6 survey.
